## 1. PURPOSE

Map the major pre-meal state transitions Baseline is intended to address and determine, for each one:

1. pre-meal state,
2. incoming meal challenge,
3. transition mismatch,
4. plausible adverse response,
5. candidate intervention mechanism,
6. evidence level,
7. whether current R1 plausibly addresses it,
8. whether a new R2 formulation would likely be required,
9. measurable outcome,
10. relevance of a water comparator,
11. safety constraints,
12. kill criterion.

This artifact does not authorize new ingredients.

---

## 2. CORE MODEL

The emerging Baseline hypothesis is:

**Meal-transition burden may depend partly on the distance between the pre-meal state and the incoming meal state.**

Candidate dimensions of transition distance:

- hydration distance
- energy-intake distance
- electrolyte distance
- macronutrient distance
- stomach-volume distance
- hunger distance
- eating-rate / behavioral distance

Baseline is intended to create a small, controlled intermediate state before eating.

This remains:

**MODELING_HYPOTHESIS**

# 3. TRANSITION MAP

## TRANSITION A — LONG FAST / LONG GAP → LARGE MIXED MEAL

### Pre-meal state
- long interval since prior food intake
- potentially high hunger
- potentially reduced fluid intake
- low recent energy intake

### Incoming meal challenge
- large caloric load
- large volume
- mixed fat/carbohydrate/protein
- possible high sodium

### Transition mismatch
**HIGH**

### Plausible adverse response
- rapid eating
- overeating
- acute fullness
- GI discomfort
- large subjective energy swing
- potentially larger post-meal glucose excursion depending on meal composition

### Candidate intervention mechanism
- standardized fluid preload
- brief delay before meal
- mild electrolyte/carbohydrate exposure
- behavioral pacing

### Evidence level
**PLAUSIBLE / PARTIALLY SUPPORTED BY PRELOAD LITERATURE**

### Does R1 address it?
**YES — PARTIALLY**

R1 plausibly addresses:
- hydration
- preload volume
- structured pause
- low-level electrolyte/carbohydrate intake

R1 does not establish:
- glucose buffering
- sodium-load buffering
- high-fat buffering

### R2 required?
**NOT NECESSARILY FOR FIRST TEST**

R1 can test whether the minimalist preload itself changes the transition.

### Measurable outcomes
- hunger before meal
- fullness before meal
- eating rate
- total meal intake
- GI comfort
- subjective post-meal comfort
- optional post-meal glucose

### Water comparator relevance
**CRITICAL**

Matched-volume water may explain much of the preload effect.

### Safety constraints
- diabetes / glucose sensitivity
- renal impairment
- sodium/potassium-sensitive conditions
- medication interactions

### Kill criterion
If R1 performs no differently from matched water across prespecified meaningful outcomes, the active-ingredient advantage for this use case is not established.

## TRANSITION B — UNDERHYDRATED → NORMAL OR DENSE MEAL

### Pre-meal state
- low recent fluid intake
- thirst
- possible mild dehydration

### Incoming meal challenge
- normal or large meal
- possibly salty/dry food

### Transition mismatch
**MODERATE TO HIGH**

### Plausible adverse response
- intensified thirst
- poor meal comfort
- possible faster drinking/eating behavior
- less standardized starting state

### Candidate intervention mechanism
- fluid preload
- modest electrolyte support
- low-level sodium-glucose-assisted absorption

### Evidence level
**PLAUSIBLE**

### Does R1 address it?
**YES — THIS IS R1'S STRONGEST CURRENT FIT**

### R2 required?
**NO FOR INITIAL TEST**

### Measurable outcomes
- thirst
- body mass / hydration proxy where appropriate
- urine concentration proxy where appropriate
- subjective hydration
- meal comfort
- water intake during meal

### Water comparator relevance
**CRITICAL**

Need to determine whether electrolytes/dextrose add value beyond 350 mL water.

### Safety constraints
- sodium-restricted conditions
- renal impairment
- potassium-sensitive medications/conditions

### Kill criterion
If matched water performs equivalently, R1's added ingredients do not earn a hydration-transition advantage.

## TRANSITION D — HIGH-SODIUM MEAL AFTER LOW-FLUID / IRREGULAR DAY

### Pre-meal state
- low fluid intake
- irregular food intake
- possible high hunger

### Incoming meal challenge
- high sodium
- restaurant / processed meal
- potentially large caloric load

### Transition mismatch
**HIGH**

### Plausible adverse response
- thirst
- fluid-shift burden
- transient vascular effects in susceptible contexts
- post-meal discomfort

### Candidate intervention mechanism
Possible future mechanisms:
- pre-hydration
- electrolyte composition specifically designed for sodium-load context
- potassium-oriented architecture if evidence and safety support it

### Evidence level
**BIOLOGICALLY PLAUSIBLE; FORMULA-SPECIFIC BENEFIT UNPROVEN**

### Does R1 address it?
**NO — NOT AS A BUFFERING FORMULA**

R1 adds approximately:
- 295 mg sodium
- 131 mg potassium

R1 cannot currently be treated as a high-sodium-meal buffering intervention.

### R2 required?
**LIKELY YES**

Only if this use case remains a priority after mechanism review.

### Measurable outcomes
- thirst
- blood pressure response where appropriate
- vascular surrogate in formal research
- post-meal comfort
- fluid intake
- possibly urinary sodium/potassium in research settings

### Water comparator relevance
**HIGH**

### Safety constraints
- renal disease
- potassium-altering medications
- sodium restriction
- cardiovascular disease
- no high-dose potassium experimentation without appropriate safety review

### Kill criterion
If a safe R2 cannot produce a credible mechanism and measurable improvement over water / control without creating safety or burden problems, this use case is dropped.

**HIGH**

# 4. R1 COVERAGE MAP

## R1 plausibly addresses

- hydration transition
- fluid preload
- behavioral pause
- high-hunger transition
- chaotic-day transition, partially
- fasting-to-meal transition, partially

## R1 does not currently establish

- sodium-load buffering
- potassium-based sodium mitigation
- glycemic buffering
- high-fat vascular buffering
- postprandial lipid attenuation
- nutritional balancing of an inadequate meal

---

# 5. R2 TRIGGER RULE

A new R2 formulation may only be authorized when:

1. a target transition cannot reasonably be addressed by R1,
2. the target transition has a plausible measurable adverse response,
3. a specific mechanism has credible evidence,
4. a low-burden intervention is technically possible,
5. the mechanism can be tested against water / R1,
6. safety constraints are acceptable,
7. product complexity remains consistent with Baseline philosophy.

No R2 ingredient may be added solely because it sounds beneficial.

# 7. FIRST EMPIRICAL QUESTION

The cleanest first test remains:

> In adults entering a meal after a prespecified food-free interval, does R1 produce a different transition response than 350 mL water alone?

Primary candidate measures:

- pre-meal thirst
- pre-meal hunger
- fullness
- eating rate
- meal duration
- meal intake
- GI comfort
- subjective post-meal comfort

Optional:
- glucose response where accessible

This experiment tests whether the active ingredients add value beyond the water preload.

---

# 8. COMMERCIAL POSITIONING BOUNDARY

Until data exist, Baseline may be described internally as:

**a minimalist pre-meal transition prototype**

Do not yet represent it as:

- a high-sodium meal buffer
- a glycemic-control product
- a vascular-protection product
- a refeeding product
- a meal-balancing product
- a metabolic stabilization treatment

# 9. DEVELOPMENT CONCLUSION

The broader Baseline theory survives.

But it is now decomposed into distinct mechanism classes rather than one oversized claim.

The project should preserve:

**one simple R1 prototype**

while separately evaluating whether any high-value transition justifies:

**one evidence-driven R2**

This prevents premature formula inflation.
