Core reading

Continuity is integrity under time. The test is whether the record, standard, direction, and repair discipline remain intact after pressure, context change, and delay.

Continuity Subfacet 01

Memory

Memory is what persists after explanation fades. It asks what remains when the speech, pressure, justification, or performance is gone.

Retained state Persistence Law Evidence Retention Law
Practical

Memory is the difference between a lesson and a performance. A person, team, or institution has memory when the standard remains after the meeting, conflict, explanation, or emotional moment has passed.

  • Personal memory: what you still do when no one is reminding you.
  • Relational memory: whether prior trust, harm, repair, and promises still count.
  • Organizational memory: whether the system remembers its own failures.
  • False memory: when a story replaces the retained record.
Engineering

Memory maps to retained state. In a platform, the question is whether state survives restart, handoff, deployment, migration, user return, failure, and audit.

  • Persist state deliberately; do not rely on temporary execution context.
  • Separate source-of-truth records from derived caches.
  • Track schema changes, migrations, and data retention boundaries.
  • Detect measurement drift when the record appears stable but meaning changes.
Evidence

Evidence for memory requires retained records, not recollection alone. A claim that something persisted should be supported by timestamped state, version history, audit trails, repeated behavior, or recoverable artifacts.

  • Strong evidence: durable logs, versioned artifacts, preserved records, repeated action.
  • Weak evidence: self-description without record.
  • Failure mode: memory is claimed, but no record survives.
  • Boundary: retained state does not automatically prove correctness.
Canonical Law Persistence Law

What matters must remain recoverable across time, pressure, and fading explanation.

Canonical Law Evidence Retention Law

A retained claim is only as strong as the record that can still be inspected.

Continuity Subfacet 02

Transfer

Transfer asks whether standards survive environment change. Integrity is weak if it only works in one room, one role, one incentive system, or one audience.

Standard survival Context Law Stability Law
Practical

Transfer is tested when the environment changes. A standard that exists only under friendly conditions is not yet continuity.

  • Role change: does the standard survive promotion, demotion, or new responsibility?
  • Incentive change: does the standard survive reward pressure?
  • Audience change: does the same integrity remain in public and private?
  • Context change: does the rule still apply when conditions are less convenient?
Engineering

Transfer maps to portability and invariance. A platform standard must survive deployment target, runtime environment, dependency change, data shape change, and interface boundary.

  • Test distribution shift rather than only ideal fixtures.
  • Preserve interface contracts across implementations.
  • Watch for environment-specific hidden assumptions.
  • Measure stable performance across contexts, not only one benchmark.
Evidence

Evidence for transfer requires cross-context observation. One successful case does not prove that a standard transfers.

  • Strong evidence: same standard holds across environments.
  • Useful evidence: context-labeled comparisons and edge cases.
  • Failure mode: performance depends on one hidden context.
  • Boundary: transfer evidence must name the environments tested.
Canonical Law Context Law

A standard must name the context in which it is valid and the conditions under which it changes.

Canonical Law Stability Law

What transfers without distortion has stronger continuity than what only works in one setting.

Continuity Subfacet 03

Trajectory

Trajectory is the direction implied by repeated decisions. It asks whether the system is moving toward repair, drift, compounding strength, or compounding failure.

Repeated direction Directional Law Compounding Law
Practical

A single event can mislead. Trajectory asks what the repeated pattern is doing. One repair can be real, but repeated avoidance tells a different story.

  • Trend versus event: do not confuse one moment with direction.
  • Drift: small repeated deviations become a new path.
  • Correction speed: slow correction can become permission for decay.
  • Compounding: repeated choices create momentum.
Engineering

Trajectory maps to time-ordered system state. It tracks trend, variance, feedback, latency, perturbation response, and whether correction is keeping pace with drift.

  • Measure time series, not isolated metrics.
  • Track directional change after perturbations.
  • Monitor feedback latency and correction lag.
  • Distinguish stable variance from directional deterioration.
Evidence

Evidence for trajectory requires ordered observations. A direction claim needs time, sequence, repeated samples, and a declared window.

  • Strong evidence: repeated records across a defined interval.
  • Useful evidence: before / after response to correction.
  • Failure mode: claiming direction from one point.
  • Boundary: trajectory does not prove final outcome; it shows current direction.
Canonical Law Directional Law

Repeated decisions disclose direction more reliably than isolated declarations.

Canonical Law Compounding Law

Small repeated choices become structural force when they continue without correction.

Continuity Subfacet 04

Decay

Decay is the quiet accumulation of uncorrected exceptions. It begins when the exception is tolerated long enough to become the new structure.

Uncorrected exception Entropy Law Maintenance Law
Practical

Decay often looks harmless at first. It begins as an exception, becomes a habit, then becomes the system’s actual rule.

  • Exception creep: the tolerated exception expands.
  • Excuse replacing repair: explanation becomes a substitute for correction.
  • Pressure accelerators: stress makes unresolved exceptions spread faster.
  • Delayed repair: the cost of correction rises over time.
Engineering

Decay maps to correction latency, entropy, drift, backlog, dependency rot, incentive inversion, and downstream amplification.

  • Track known exceptions as debt, not background noise.
  • Measure correction latency and recurrence.
  • Detect when manual override becomes normal operation.
  • Watch for downstream amplification from unresolved defects.
Evidence

Evidence for decay requires a record of unresolved exceptions over time. The key question is not whether a defect existed, but whether it remained uncorrected long enough to alter behavior.

  • Strong evidence: recurring exception logs and delayed repair records.
  • Useful evidence: known issue age, recurrence, and downstream impact.
  • Failure mode: treating exceptions as isolated incidents.
  • Boundary: decay evidence must separate cause, delay, and consequence.
Canonical Law Entropy Law

Uncorrected disorder tends to spread into the surrounding structure.

Canonical Law Maintenance Law

What is not maintained will eventually be governed by the cost of neglect.
