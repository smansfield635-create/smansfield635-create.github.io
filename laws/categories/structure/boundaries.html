Structure Track · Boundaries

What separates domains.

Boundaries define what belongs inside, what must remain outside, what may cross, and what must never leak. They protect identity, containment, permission, and domain integrity.

Track standing

Boundaries is one Structure track in the sixteen-track Laws constellation. It sits after Interfaces and before Governance.

  • Constraints limit what can happen.
  • Interfaces define where exchange occurs.
  • Boundaries define separation and controlled crossing.
  • Governance keeps the rules active under pressure.
Boundary signature

A strong boundary has clear separation, declared crossing rules, leakage detection, and containment when failure occurs.

Boundaries Section 01

Separation

Defines what belongs to one domain and what does not.

Domain identity Scope line Responsibility edge Inside / outside
Platform

Separation is the first act of boundary integrity. It says what belongs here, what belongs elsewhere, and what cannot be treated as the same thing.

  • Name the domain before assigning responsibility.
  • Separate ownership from adjacency.
  • Do not merge unlike categories because they touch.
  • Prevent pressure from erasing the line.
Engineering

Separation maps to namespaces, scopes, modules, access domains, service ownership, tenant isolation, environment boundaries, and trust zones.

  • Declare scope explicitly.
  • Separate public, private, internal, and protected domains.
  • Keep ownership boundaries visible in code, data, and operations.
  • Prevent one domain from silently inheriting another domain’s responsibility.
Evidence

Evidence for separation requires visible domain lines and records showing that the lines are enforced.

  • Strong evidence: access maps, ownership maps, scope records, environment separation, domain diagrams.
  • Useful evidence: policy boundaries, module boundaries, access logs, ownership receipts.
  • Failure mode: adjacent domains are treated as interchangeable.
  • Boundary: separation is not isolation; it is controlled identity preservation.
Boundaries Section 02

Crossing Rule

Defines what may pass the boundary, under which condition, and with what preserved accountability.

Permission Gate condition Transfer rule Boundary crossing
Platform

Crossing rules explain what may pass the boundary, under which condition, with whose permission, and with what preserved accountability.

  • Define what may cross.
  • Define who authorizes crossing.
  • Define what must be transformed, logged, or rejected.
  • Do not allow urgency to replace permission.
Engineering

Crossing rules map to access control, authorization gates, firewall rules, API permissions, validation policy, data transfer rules, and domain transition states.

  • Gate crossing through declared permission checks.
  • Validate before transfer.
  • Log cross-boundary movement.
  • Preserve provenance when data changes domain.
Evidence

Evidence for crossing quality requires proof that allowed crossings are governed and rejected crossings are handled deliberately.

  • Strong evidence: access logs, authorization records, transfer receipts, rejected crossing records.
  • Useful evidence: permission maps, policy tests, boundary transition traces.
  • Failure mode: crossing happens because systems are adjacent, not because permission exists.
  • Boundary: a crossing rule can permit exchange without collapsing domain separation.
Boundaries Section 03

Leakage Control

Detects boundary failure without declared crossing.

Leak detection Unowned transfer Contamination Scope creep
Platform

Leakage is not always dramatic. It often begins as a small tolerated transfer: a responsibility pushed into the wrong room, a risk hidden in another layer, or an exception normalized at the edge.

  • Detect responsibility moving without ownership.
  • Detect private pressure changing public structure.
  • Detect risk moved downstream without disclosure.
  • Detect scope expansion without boundary revision.
Engineering

Leakage maps to data exfiltration, privilege bleed, state bleed, dependency contamination, cross-tenant exposure, scope creep, configuration drift, and implicit coupling.

  • Monitor unauthorized domain movement.
  • Detect privilege or state bleed across scopes.
  • Prevent hidden coupling between separated systems.
  • Treat repeated leakage as boundary erosion.
Evidence

Evidence for leakage control requires both detection and response. A boundary is weak if it notices leakage but never repairs the cause.

  • Strong evidence: leakage logs, boundary violation records, contamination traces, repair verification.
  • Useful evidence: scope-change logs, access anomalies, downstream exposure reports.
  • Failure mode: leakage is reframed as normal collaboration.
  • Boundary: lawful crossing is not leakage when permission, provenance, and containment are preserved.
Boundaries Section 04

Containment

Prevents boundary failure from becoming system failure.

Spread limit Recovery space Blast radius Isolation under failure
Platform

Containment is the boundary’s emergency function. When something fails, containment keeps the damage from spreading beyond the domain that can repair it.

  • Limit spread before explaining failure.
  • Preserve recovery space.
  • Prevent blame, risk, or defect migration into unrelated domains.
  • Keep the repairable problem from becoming a system-wide condition.
Engineering

Containment maps to sandboxing, isolation, circuit breakers, rate limits, blast-radius reduction, rollback boundaries, access revocation, quarantine, and fail-closed behavior.

  • Define blast radius before failure occurs.
  • Use isolation to stop propagation.
  • Fail closed where unsafe spread is possible.
  • Restore only after recovery conditions are met.
Evidence

Evidence for containment requires records that show spread was limited and recovery remained possible.

  • Strong evidence: incident boundary, blast-radius record, quarantine log, rollback receipt, recovery verification.
  • Useful evidence: rate-limit records, isolation tests, failover drills, restored-state checks.
  • Failure mode: one domain fails and silently contaminates the rest.
  • Boundary: containment is not denial; it is controlled limitation of spread.
Canonical Boundary Laws

What cannot be separated cannot be protected.

Open for laws, signatures, and failure patterns.

Boundaries hold structure by preserving domain identity, controlling crossing, detecting leakage, and containing failure before it becomes systemic.

Canonical Law Boundary Preservation Law

A domain remains stable only while its identity, responsibility, and crossing rules remain distinguishable.

Canonical Law Leakage Detection Law

Undeclared transfer across a boundary is structural leakage until permission, provenance, and containment are restored.

Signature Protected separation

Clear domain line, governed crossing, leakage detection, and containment under failure.

Failure Pattern Boundary erosion

Pressure, risk, responsibility, data, or authority moves across domains without declared permission.
