01 / 04

Separation

  1. Domain identity

    Practical
    Separation is the first act of boundary integrity. It says what belongs here, what belongs elsewhere, and what cannot be treated as the same thing. Name the domain before assigning responsibility.
    Engineering
    Separation maps to namespaces, scopes, modules, access domains, service ownership, tenant isolation, environment boundaries, and trust zones. Declare scope explicitly.
    Empirical
    For separation requires visible domain lines and records showing that the lines are enforced. Strong evidence: access maps, ownership maps, scope records, environment separation, domain diagrams.

  2. Scope line

    Practical
    Separation is the first act of boundary integrity. It says what belongs here, what belongs elsewhere, and what cannot be treated as the same thing. Separate ownership from adjacency.
    Engineering
    Separation maps to namespaces, scopes, modules, access domains, service ownership, tenant isolation, environment boundaries, and trust zones. Separate public, private, internal, and protected domains.
    Empirical
    For separation requires visible domain lines and records showing that the lines are enforced. Useful evidence: policy boundaries, module boundaries, access logs, ownership receipts.

  3. Responsibility edge

    Practical
    Separation is the first act of boundary integrity. It says what belongs here, what belongs elsewhere, and what cannot be treated as the same thing. Do not merge unlike categories because they touch.
    Engineering
    Separation maps to namespaces, scopes, modules, access domains, service ownership, tenant isolation, environment boundaries, and trust zones. Keep ownership boundaries visible in code, data, and operations.
    Empirical
    For separation requires visible domain lines and records showing that the lines are enforced. Failure mode: adjacent domains are treated as interchangeable.

  4. Inside / outside

    Practical
    Separation is the first act of boundary integrity. It says what belongs here, what belongs elsewhere, and what cannot be treated as the same thing. Prevent pressure from erasing the line.
    Engineering
    Separation maps to namespaces, scopes, modules, access domains, service ownership, tenant isolation, environment boundaries, and trust zones. Prevent one domain from silently inheriting another domain’s responsibility.
    Empirical
    For separation requires visible domain lines and records showing that the lines are enforced. Boundary: separation is not isolation; it is controlled identity preservation. Claim boundary: Separation is not isolation; it is controlled identity preservation.

02 / 04

Crossing

  1. Permission

    Practical
    Crossing rules explain what may pass the boundary, under which condition, with whose permission, and with what preserved accountability. Define what may cross.
    Engineering
    Crossing rules map to access control, authorization gates, firewall rules, API permissions, validation policy, data transfer rules, and domain transition states. Gate crossing through declared permission checks.
    Empirical
    For crossing quality requires proof that allowed crossings are governed and rejected crossings are handled deliberately. Strong evidence: access logs, authorization records, transfer receipts, rejected crossing records.

  2. Gate condition

    Practical
    Crossing rules explain what may pass the boundary, under which condition, with whose permission, and with what preserved accountability. Define who authorizes crossing.
    Engineering
    Crossing rules map to access control, authorization gates, firewall rules, API permissions, validation policy, data transfer rules, and domain transition states. Validate before transfer.
    Empirical
    For crossing quality requires proof that allowed crossings are governed and rejected crossings are handled deliberately. Useful evidence: permission maps, policy tests, boundary transition traces.

  3. Transfer rule

    Practical
    Crossing rules explain what may pass the boundary, under which condition, with whose permission, and with what preserved accountability. Define what must be transformed, logged, or rejected.
    Engineering
    Crossing rules map to access control, authorization gates, firewall rules, API permissions, validation policy, data transfer rules, and domain transition states. Log cross-boundary movement.
    Empirical
    For crossing quality requires proof that allowed crossings are governed and rejected crossings are handled deliberately. Failure mode: crossing happens because systems are adjacent, not because permission exists.

  4. Boundary crossing

    Practical
    Crossing rules explain what may pass the boundary, under which condition, with whose permission, and with what preserved accountability. Do not allow urgency to replace permission.
    Engineering
    Crossing rules map to access control, authorization gates, firewall rules, API permissions, validation policy, data transfer rules, and domain transition states. Preserve provenance when data changes domain.
    Empirical
    For crossing quality requires proof that allowed crossings are governed and rejected crossings are handled deliberately. Boundary: a crossing rule can permit exchange without collapsing domain separation. Claim boundary: A crossing rule can permit exchange without collapsing domain separation.

03 / 04

Leakage

  1. Leak detection

    Practical
    Leakage is not always dramatic. It often begins as a small tolerated transfer: a responsibility pushed into the wrong room, a risk hidden in another layer, or an exception normalized at the edge. Detect responsibility moving without ownership.
    Engineering
    Leakage maps to data exfiltration, privilege bleed, state bleed, dependency contamination, cross-tenant exposure, scope creep, configuration drift, and implicit coupling. Monitor unauthorized domain movement.
    Empirical
    For leakage control requires both detection and response. A boundary is weak if it notices leakage but never repairs the cause. Strong evidence: leakage logs, boundary violation records, contamination traces, repair verification.

  2. Unowned transfer

    Practical
    Leakage is not always dramatic. It often begins as a small tolerated transfer: a responsibility pushed into the wrong room, a risk hidden in another layer, or an exception normalized at the edge. Detect private pressure changing public structure.
    Engineering
    Leakage maps to data exfiltration, privilege bleed, state bleed, dependency contamination, cross-tenant exposure, scope creep, configuration drift, and implicit coupling. Detect privilege or state bleed across scopes.
    Empirical
    For leakage control requires both detection and response. A boundary is weak if it notices leakage but never repairs the cause. Useful evidence: scope-change logs, access anomalies, downstream exposure reports.

  3. Contamination

    Practical
    Leakage is not always dramatic. It often begins as a small tolerated transfer: a responsibility pushed into the wrong room, a risk hidden in another layer, or an exception normalized at the edge. Detect risk moved downstream without disclosure.
    Engineering
    Leakage maps to data exfiltration, privilege bleed, state bleed, dependency contamination, cross-tenant exposure, scope creep, configuration drift, and implicit coupling. Prevent hidden coupling between separated systems.
    Empirical
    For leakage control requires both detection and response. A boundary is weak if it notices leakage but never repairs the cause. Failure mode: leakage is reframed as normal collaboration.

  4. Scope creep

    Practical
    Leakage is not always dramatic. It often begins as a small tolerated transfer: a responsibility pushed into the wrong room, a risk hidden in another layer, or an exception normalized at the edge. Detect scope expansion without boundary revision.
    Engineering
    Leakage maps to data exfiltration, privilege bleed, state bleed, dependency contamination, cross-tenant exposure, scope creep, configuration drift, and implicit coupling. Treat repeated leakage as boundary erosion.
    Empirical
    For leakage control requires both detection and response. A boundary is weak if it notices leakage but never repairs the cause. Boundary: lawful crossing is not leakage when permission, provenance, and containment are preserved. Claim boundary: Is weak if it notices leakage but never repairs the cause. 04 / 04

Containment

  1. Spread limit

    Practical
    Containment is the boundary’s emergency function. When something fails, containment keeps the damage from spreading beyond the domain that can repair it. Limit spread before explaining failure.
    Engineering
    Containment maps to sandboxing, isolation, circuit breakers, rate limits, blast-radius reduction, rollback boundaries, access revocation, quarantine, and fail-closed behavior. Define blast radius before failure occurs.
    Empirical
    For containment requires records that show spread was limited and recovery remained possible. Strong evidence: incident boundary, blast-radius record, quarantine log, rollback receipt, recovery verification.

  2. Recovery space

    Practical
    Containment is the boundary’s emergency function. When something fails, containment keeps the damage from spreading beyond the domain that can repair it. Preserve recovery space.
    Engineering
    Containment maps to sandboxing, isolation, circuit breakers, rate limits, blast-radius reduction, rollback boundaries, access revocation, quarantine, and fail-closed behavior. Use isolation to stop propagation.
    Empirical
    For containment requires records that show spread was limited and recovery remained possible. Useful evidence: rate-limit records, isolation tests, failover drills, restored-state checks.

  3. Blast radius

    Practical
    Containment is the boundary’s emergency function. When something fails, containment keeps the damage from spreading beyond the domain that can repair it. Prevent blame, risk, or defect migration into unrelated domains.
    Engineering
    Containment maps to sandboxing, isolation, circuit breakers, rate limits, blast-radius reduction, rollback boundaries, access revocation, quarantine, and fail-closed behavior. Fail closed where unsafe spread is possible.
    Empirical
    For containment requires records that show spread was limited and recovery remained possible. Failure mode: one domain fails and silently contaminates the rest.

  4. Isolation under failure

    Practical
    Containment is the boundary’s emergency function. When something fails, containment keeps the damage from spreading beyond the domain that can repair it. Keep the repairable problem from becoming a system-wide condition.
    Engineering
    Containment maps to sandboxing, isolation, circuit breakers, rate limits, blast-radius reduction, rollback boundaries, access revocation, quarantine, and fail-closed behavior. Restore only after recovery conditions are met.
    Empirical
    For containment requires records that show spread was limited and recovery remained possible. Boundary: containment is not denial; it is controlled limitation of spread. Claim boundary: Containment is not denial; it is controlled limitation of spread.
