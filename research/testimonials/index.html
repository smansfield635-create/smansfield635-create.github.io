The story behind the study

Nobody wakes up excited about gutters.

They wake up thinking about the house they have worked to build, the repairs they would rather avoid, and the small problems that somehow keep coming back.

That is what I began to recognize during my first 100 sales in the field. From one home to the next, the circumstances changed. Some homeowners were frustrated with leaves and clogs. Others were dealing with failing screens, damaged wood, or the worry that water might reach the foundation. Some had questions about how a new system would fit their house or look when the work was finished.

On paper, these might look like different product questions. Across the kitchen table, they were something more personal. What will happen the next time it rains? Am I going to have to deal with this again? Is this the right decision for my home?

Those questions changed how I thought about my work. I wasn't there just to describe gutters or recite features. I had to understand what the homeowner was actually trying to solve, explain the choices clearly, and give them room to make a decision they could feel good about.

The lesson wasn't that every homeowner wanted the same thing. It was that every homeowner wanted to be understood.

I began documenting my sales to better understand my own performance and the concerns I encountered. Looking across the surviving notes, certain subjects kept surfacing: recurring debris, damage and attachments, difficult corners, and the appearance of a finished home.

This page shares those observations, then gives you a way to meet the people behind them. You can follow a particular concern or browse the customer notes in their original numbered order.

These records are brief field notes, not full interviews or verbatim customer testimonials. The words in them describe what I recorded, not a controlled test of why a person decided to buy.

Beyond the product

What does a lasting promise mean?

Some conversations raised concerns about clogs, attachments, paint finish, or the small details that make an installation look complete. These concerns also appear in the warranty language associated with the product I was selling. A promise is only useful when a homeowner understands what the written terms actually cover.

These are historical topic labels, not an interpretation of coverage or a statement about current terms. Consult the actual written warranty.
